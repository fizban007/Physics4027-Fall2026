Part I · Multistep methods

Reusing the past

A Runge-Kutta step forgets

  • RK4 evaluates $f$ four times per step, combines the slopes, and throws them away. The next step starts from $y_{n+1}$ alone.
  • But the slopes of earlier steps, $f_{n-1} = f(t_{n-1}, y_{n-1})$, $f_{n-2}$, and so on, are good information about how $f$ is changing.
  • A multistep method keeps the last few slopes and builds $y_{n+1}$ from them. Each step then needs only one new evaluation, $f_n$.

Runge-Kutta methods are called one-step methods: $y_{n+1}$ depends on $y_n$ only.

Where the coefficients come from

Integrate the ODE over one step, then replace $f$ by the polynomial through the stored slopes:

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} f\big(t, y(t)\big)\,dt$$
DERIVATION (two points, AB2): the line through (t_{n-1}, f_{n-1}) and (t_n, f_n) is p(t) = f_n + (f_n - f_{n-1})(t - t_n)/h. Integrating from t_n to t_n + h gives h f_n + (f_n - f_{n-1}) h/2 = h (3/2 f_n - 1/2 f_{n-1}). The same recipe with 3 and 4 points gives AB3 and AB4. The polynomial is extrapolated over the new step, which is why the weights grow and alternate in sign.

The Adams-Bashforth methods

$$\begin{split} \text{AB2:}\quad y_{n+1} &= y_n + \frac{h}{2}\left(3 f_n - f_{n-1}\right) \\ \text{AB3:}\quad y_{n+1} &= y_n + \frac{h}{12}\left(23 f_n - 16 f_{n-1} + 5 f_{n-2}\right) \\ \text{AB4:}\quad y_{n+1} &= y_n + \frac{h}{24}\left(55 f_n - 59 f_{n-1} + 37 f_{n-2} - 9 f_{n-3}\right) \end{split}$$
  • $k$ stored slopes give order $k$. AB1 is Euler's method.
  • Explicit, and one new evaluation of $f$ per step, whatever the order.

Adams-Moulton: include the new point

Let the interpolating polynomial pass through $f_{n+1}$ as well, which interpolates instead of extrapolating:

$$\text{AM4:}\quad y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{24}\left(9 f_{n+1} + 19 f_n - 5 f_{n-1} + f_{n-2}\right)$$
  • More accurate at the same order: the local error is $\tfrac{19}{720} h^5 y^{(5)}$, against $\tfrac{251}{720} h^5 y^{(5)}$ for AB4.
  • But $f_{n+1} = f(t_{n+1}, y_{n+1})$ contains the unknown: AM4 is implicit, like implicit Euler in Lecture 7.
  • Implicit does not mean unconditionally stable: AM4 is stable only for $h\lambda \ge -3$ on the real axis. No multistep method of order above 2 is stable for every $h\lambda$ in the left half plane (Dahlquist).
DERIVATION: same recipe as Adams-Bashforth, with the cubic through f_{n-2}, f_{n-1}, f_n, f_{n+1}. The error constants 251/720 and 19/720 are the standard ones (Hairer, Norsett & Wanner I, Section III.1); they come from integrating the interpolation remainder over the step. STABILITY: largest root of AM4's characteristic polynomial on the real axis: 0.41 at h lambda = -1, 1.00 at -3, 1.74 at -10, and it tends to 2.37 as h lambda -> -infinity. Dahlquist's second barrier: an A-stable linear multistep method has order at most 2, and the trapezoidal rule (Adams-Moulton of order 2) is the best one. BDF3 and up are stable on the whole negative real axis but not near the imaginary axis (BDF3 reaches 1.05 there), which is why they are still fine for stiff problems.

Predictor-corrector

Avoid solving for $y_{n+1}$: guess it explicitly, then use the guess in the implicit formula.

  1. Predict $\tilde y_{n+1}$ with AB4.
  2. Evaluate $\tilde f_{n+1} = f(t_{n+1}, \tilde y_{n+1})$.
  3. Correct: AM4 with $\tilde f_{n+1}$ in place of $f_{n+1}$.
  4. Evaluate $f_{n+1}$ at the corrected $y_{n+1}$, for use in later steps.
  • "PECE": two evaluations per step.
  • $|y_{n+1} - \tilde y_{n+1}|$ estimates the local error for free, like the embedded pair in Dormand-Prince (Lecture 8).

Cost vs accuracy

The $170°$ pendulum over one period, fixed $h$. At about 400 evaluations of $f$:

RK4$1.2 \times 10^{-4}$
AB4$2.6 \times 10^{-5}$
AB4 + AM4$2.4 \times 10^{-4}$

All three are fourth order. Per evaluation, AB4 wins by a factor of about 4 here.

The corrector costs a second evaluation per step; what it buys is the error estimate and better stability, not accuracy per evaluation.

The multistep runs start with three RK4 steps (the start-up problem on the next slide); their cost is included. At about 1600 evaluations: RK4 1.0e-7, AB4 2.5e-8, predictor-corrector 2.5e-7. Measured slopes are a bit steeper than 4 (5.0 to 5.3): at 170 degrees the runs are not fully in the asymptotic regime.

The catch

  • Start-up. AB4 needs three earlier slopes that do not exist at $t_0$. The first steps are taken with a one-step method, such as RK4.
  • Fixed step. The weights assume equally spaced slopes. Changing $h$ means re-interpolating the history, so step control is harder than in Dormand-Prince.
  • Stability shrinks with order. Largest stable $|h\lambda|$ on the negative real axis:

    Even per evaluation, RK4 ($2.79/4 = 0.70$) is more stable than AB4.

The same analysis as the amplification factor R(z) in Lecture 7, except that a k-step method has k roots instead of one.

Backward differentiation formulas

For stiff problems, turn the idea around: interpolate the stored values $y_{n+1}, y_n, y_{n-1}, \dots$, and require the polynomial's slope at $t_{n+1}$ to equal $f_{n+1}$.

$$\begin{split} \text{BDF1:}\quad & y_{n+1} - y_n = h f_{n+1} \\ \text{BDF2:}\quad & y_{n+1} - \tfrac{4}{3} y_n + \tfrac{1}{3} y_{n-1} = \tfrac{2}{3} h f_{n+1} \end{split}$$
  • BDF1 is implicit Euler, from Homework 4.
  • Implicit and stable far into the left half plane: the standard stiff solvers (scipy's solve_ivp(method="BDF"), CVODE) use BDF of orders 1 to 5, changing order and step as they go.
DERIVATION of BDF2: the quadratic through (t_{n-1}, y_{n-1}), (t_n, y_n), (t_{n+1}, y_{n+1}) has derivative at t_{n+1} equal to (3 y_{n+1} - 4 y_n + y_{n-1}) / (2h). Setting that equal to f_{n+1} and multiplying by 2h/3 gives the formula. BDF is zero-stable only up to order 6, which is why production codes stop at 5 or 6.

Part II · Energy conservation

Long runs

Small errors, many periods

  • Homework 4: Euler pushed the pendulum over the top within one swing, while RK4 lost only $2.5 \times 10^{-11}$ of energy in two periods.
  • Many simulations run for far longer than two periods: planetary orbits over billions of revolutions, molecular dynamics over millions of steps.
  • A tiny energy error per period, repeated a million times, is no longer tiny.

The question is not only how large the error is after one period, but whether it keeps growing.

1000 periods of a pendulum

$\theta_0 = 90°$, $h = T/50$.

  • RK2 gains energy until the pendulum spins over the top.
  • RK4 loses about $4 \times 10^{-6}$ per period, steadily: $4 \times 10^{-3}$ after 1000.
  • Leapfrog (next slides) has a larger error at first, which never grows.
  • At the same cost as RK4, leapfrog is better after about 70 periods, and the gap widens forever.
The curves show the largest |E - E0| within each period, so the oscillation of the error inside a period does not clutter the log scale. Leapfrog at h: 4.6e-3 in every period. Leapfrog at h/4, one evaluation per step, same evaluations as RK4 at h: 2.9e-4 in every period. RK4: 5.8e-6, 4.4e-5, 4.2e-4, 4.2e-3 in periods 1, 10, 100, 1000. RK2 reaches 12 by period 1000.

From midpoint to leapfrog

The midpoint method (Lecture 6) computes a value at the half step, uses it once, and throws it away. Keep it instead, and let positions and velocities alternate:

$$v_{n+1/2} = v_{n-1/2} + h\, a(x_n), \qquad x_{n+1} = x_n + h\, v_{n+1/2}$$

Each update uses the other variable at the midpoint of its own step: second order, and one evaluation of the force $a(x)$ per step.

DERIVATION of second order: each update is the midpoint rule for its own variable, dx/dt = v evaluated at the midpoint of [t_n, t_{n+1}], and dv/dt = a evaluated at the midpoint of [t_{n-1/2}, t_{n+1/2}]; the midpoint rule's error per step is O(h^3). The method is also symmetric in time (two slides on), and symmetric methods have even order.

Velocity Verlet

For $\ddot x = a(x)$, the same method with positions and velocities reported at the same times: half a kick, a drift, half a kick.

$$\begin{split} v_{n+1/2} &= v_n + \tfrac{h}{2}\, a(x_n) \\ x_{n+1} &= x_n + h\, v_{n+1/2} \\ v_{n+1} &= v_{n+1/2} + \tfrac{h}{2}\, a(x_{n+1}) \end{split}$$
  • $a(x_{n+1})$ is reused in the first half kick of the next step: still one evaluation per step.
  • This is the workhorse of molecular dynamics, where it is called velocity Verlet.

Leapfrog runs backwards exactly

  • Every substep is undone by the same substep with $-h$: $x_{n+1} = x_n + h\,v$ is reversed by $x_n = x_{n+1} - h\,v$, with the same $v$.
  • So reversing the velocity and running the same number of steps retraces the trajectory, as the exact dynamics does.
  • Measured at $\theta_0 = 90°$, $h = 0.1$: run 1000 steps, flip $v$, run 1000 more.
    distance from the startRK4 $2.1 \times 10^{-4}$
    leapfrog$6.3 \times 10^{-14}$

Leapfrog's only error on the way back is rounding.

RK4's stages are all evaluated from the start of the step, so the backward step is a different method from the forward one and does not undo it. Time reversibility alone does not guarantee bounded energy, but it is one of the two properties (with the area preservation on the next slides) that leapfrog has and RK methods lack.

Hamiltonian structure

The pendulum, like every conservative mechanical system, is a Hamiltonian system:

$$H(q, p) = \frac{p^2}{2} + V(q), \qquad \dot q = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p} = p, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q} = -V'(q)$$

with $V(q) = -\cos q$ for the pendulum. Two consequences:

  • Energy is conserved: $\dot H = \frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\dot q + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\dot p = \frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q} = 0$.
  • Phase-space area is conserved (Liouville's theorem): the velocity field $(\dot q, \dot p)$ has zero divergence, $\frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial q} + \frac{\partial \dot p}{\partial p} = \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q\,\partial p} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p\,\partial q} = 0$.

A numerical method can try to keep either property. Leapfrog keeps the second one exactly.

Zero divergence means the flow neither compresses nor expands a patch of initial conditions, like an incompressible fluid in the (q, p) plane. In 2D, "area"; in general, phase-space volume. For the pendulum, q = theta and p = theta-dot (unit mass and length).

Leapfrog is built from exact solutions

Split $H = T(p) + V(q)$, with $T = p^2/2$, and solve each half on its own:

Hamiltonianequationsexact solution for a time $\tau$
$V(q)$ alone $\dot q = 0,\; \dot p = -V'(q)$ $p \to p - \tau V'(q)$: a kick
$T(p)$ alone $\dot q = p,\; \dot p = 0$ $q \to q + \tau p$: a drift
  • A Verlet step is the exact flow of $V$ for $h/2$, then of $T$ for $h$, then of $V$ for $h/2$.
  • Each piece is an exact Hamiltonian flow, so each preserves phase-space area, and so does the whole step.
  • Euler moves $q$ and $p$ at once, both from the old state: it is not the exact flow of anything.
This is the idea of splitting methods: when H is a sum of pieces that can each be solved exactly, alternate between them. The error of leapfrog comes entirely from the fact that the flows of T and V do not commute (kick then drift is not drift then kick).

Area and energy on the harmonic oscillator

On $\ddot x = -x$ every method here multiplies $(x, v)$ by a matrix $M$, and the energy curves $x^2 + v^2 = 2E$ are circles. A Runge-Kutta step rotates and rescales, so it multiplies the area and the energy by $\det M$, every step:

$\det M$ per step$1 + h^2$$1 + h^4/4$ $1 - h^6/72 + \dots$$1$
energygrowsgrows decaysbounded
  • For Runge-Kutta, the energy error is the area error: $\det M = |R(ih)|^2$, with $R$ the amplification factor of Lecture 7, close to 1 but never equal to it.
  • Leapfrog's $M$ has determinant exactly 1 but is not a rotation: it carries circles to ellipses of the same area. $E$ wobbles; the shadow energy $\tfrac12 v^2 + \tfrac12\left(1 - \tfrac{h^2}{4}\right)x^2$ is conserved exactly.
DERIVATION: on x'' = -x the system is y' = A y with A = [[0, 1], [-1, 0]], and a Runge-Kutta step is y -> R(hA) y. Since A^2 = -1, R(hA) = Re R(ih) I + Im R(ih) A: a rotation times |R(ih)|. So |y|^2, and with it E, is multiplied by |R(ih)|^2 = det M each step. RK2: R = 1 + z + z^2/2, |R(ih)|^2 = (1 - h^2/2)^2 + h^2 = 1 + h^4/4. RK4: |R(ih)|^2 = 1 - h^6/72 + h^8/576. Euler: 1 + h^2. Measured determinants at h = 0.1: 1.0100000000, 1.0000250000, 0.9999999861, 1.0000000000. Leapfrog: M = [[1 - h^2/2, h], [-h(1 - h^2/4), 1 - h^2/2]], and M^T Q M = Q with Q = diag(1 - h^2/4, 1); measured at h = 0.5 over 1000 steps, E varies by 3e-2 and the shadow energy by 3e-15. This is the V = q^2/2 case of the H-tilde on the next slide.

Why the energy error stays bounded

  • Kicks and drifts do not commute, so a Verlet step is not the exact flow of $H$. But it is the exact flow of a nearby ("shadow") Hamiltonian: $$\tilde H = H + \frac{h^2}{12}\, p^2\, V''(q) - \frac{h^2}{24}\, V'(q)^2 + O(h^4)$$
  • An exact Hamiltonian flow conserves its own Hamiltonian. So leapfrog conserves $\tilde H$, and $H = \tilde H + O(h^2)$ can oscillate but never drift.
  • Measured on the $90°$ pendulum over 200 time units: at $h = 0.2$, $H$ varies by $8.3 \times 10^{-3}$ and $\tilde H$ by $7.0 \times 10^{-5}$; at $h = 0.1$, by $2.1 \times 10^{-3}$ and $4.4 \times 10^{-6}$.
  • RK4 is not the exact flow of any Hamiltonian: no $\tilde H$ exists, and its error accumulates as the steady drift seen earlier.
DERIVATION sketch. Each substep is exp(tau X) for the Hamiltonian vector field X of T or V. The Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula writes exp(h/2 A) exp(h B) exp(h/2 A) = exp(h (A + B) + h^3 C + ...), where C is built from commutators of A and B; commutators of Hamiltonian vector fields are again Hamiltonian, so the whole series is the vector field of a single H-tilde. Its h^2 term for velocity Verlet is the one on the slide; I checked the coefficients numerically (make_figures.py): the variation of H-tilde falls by 16 when h halves, as the O(h^4) remainder requires. The series does not converge in general, but truncating it optimally gives conservation for exponentially long times (Hairer, Lubich & Wanner, Chapter IX). Harmonic oscillator check: V = q^2/2 gives H-tilde proportional to p^2/2 + (1 - h^2/4) q^2/2, which leapfrog conserves exactly (M^T Q M = Q with Q = diag(1 - h^2/4, 1)).

The fine print

  • Fixed step. Changing $h$ from step to step (adaptive control) changes the shadow Hamiltonian as you go, and the bounded error is lost.
  • Hamiltonian systems that split. Leapfrog needs $H = T(p) + V(q)$, two pieces each solved exactly. With friction or a driving force there is no energy to conserve; when $H$ mixes $q$ and $p$ (a charged particle in a magnetic field), a different splitting is needed.
  • Second order. Higher orders come from composing leapfrog steps: three substeps of sizes $w_1 h$, $w_0 h$, $w_1 h$ with $w_1 = 1/(2 - 2^{1/3}) \approx 1.35$ and $w_0 = 1 - 2 w_1 \approx -1.70$ give a fourth-order symplectic method (Yoshida 1990).
  • Not more accurate. Over a few periods RK4 wins, as the figure showed. Symplectic methods win over long runs.
