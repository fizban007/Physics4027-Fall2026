Part I · The Boris pusher

Charged particles

A charged particle in a magnetic field

$$\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{v}, \qquad \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \frac{q}{m}\left(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\right)$$
  • With $\mathbf{E} = 0$ the magnetic force is always perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$: it does no work, and $|\mathbf{v}|$ never changes.
  • In a uniform $\mathbf{B}$ the particle circles at the cyclotron frequency $\omega_c = qB/m$.
  • A good method should keep $|\mathbf{v}|$ exactly, over millions of gyrations.

Leapfrog for the Lorentz force

Stagger as in Lecture 10: positions at whole steps, velocities at half steps.

$$\begin{split} \mathbf{x}_{n+1} &= \mathbf{x}_n + \Delta t\, \mathbf{v}_{n+1/2} \\ \frac{\mathbf{v}_{n+1/2} - \mathbf{v}_{n-1/2}}{\Delta t} &= \frac{q}{m}\left(\mathbf{E}_n + \frac{\mathbf{v}_{n+1/2} + \mathbf{v}_{n-1/2}}{2}\times\mathbf{B}_n\right) \end{split}$$
  • The force depends on the velocity, which lives at half steps, so it is averaged over the step.
  • The unknown $\mathbf{v}_{n+1/2}$ appears on both sides: the update looks implicit.

Boris's trick (1970)

Half an electric kick, a rotation about $\mathbf{B}$, and another half kick:

$$\begin{split} \mathbf{v}^- &= \mathbf{v}_{n-1/2} + \frac{q\mathbf{E}}{m}\frac{\Delta t}{2} &\qquad& \mathbf{t} = \frac{q\mathbf{B}}{m}\frac{\Delta t}{2}, \quad \mathbf{s} = \frac{2\mathbf{t}}{1 + t^2} \\ \mathbf{v}^+ &= \mathbf{v}^- + \left(\mathbf{v}^- + \mathbf{v}^-\times\mathbf{t}\right)\times\mathbf{s} &\qquad& \mathbf{v}_{n+1/2} = \mathbf{v}^+ + \frac{q\mathbf{E}}{m}\frac{\Delta t}{2} \end{split}$$
  • Two cross products solve the "implicit" equation exactly: no iteration, no matrix.
  • The middle step is an exact rotation, so $|\mathbf{v}^+| = |\mathbf{v}^-|$: with $\mathbf{E} = 0$ the energy is conserved to rounding.
DERIVATION: with E = 0 the averaged equation is v+ - v- = (v+ + v-) x t. Writing T for the matrix of (. x t), this is (1 - T) v+ = (1 + T) v-, and since T is antisymmetric, (1 - T)^{-1}(1 + T) is orthogonal (a Cayley transform): a rotation about B. The two cross products compute exactly that rotation; check by substitution. The rotation angle theta satisfies tan(theta/2) = |t| = omega_c dt/2, slightly less than omega_c dt: the gyration is a little slow, but always on the right circle. Boris preserves phase-space volume (Qin et al. 2013, "Why is Boris algorithm so good?"), which is why it behaves like the symplectic methods of Lecture 10.

Boris against RK4, uniform field

  • $\omega_c \Delta t = 0.3$, about 21 steps per gyration, 1000 gyrations.
  • RK4 loses a factor $1 - (\omega_c\Delta t)^6/72$ of energy per step (Lecture 10): 19% by the end. Boris: $|\mathbf{v}|$ exact to $10^{-13}$.
Predicted RK4 energy after 20943 steps: (1 - 0.3^6/72)^20943 = 0.809; measured 0.811. Boris |v| after the run: 1.0000000000000944. Boris turns by 2 atan(0.15) = 0.29778 per step instead of 0.3.

A proton in the Van Allen belt

period$0.14$ s$2.3$ s$1.2$ min
Earth's dipole: B0 = 3.07e-5 T at the equatorial surface, falling as 1/r^3. Relativistic Boris (gamma = 1.0107 is constant in a pure magnetic field, so q/m becomes q/(gamma m)), dt = T_gyro/30, 16280 steps for 75 s, 0.7 s of wall time in Python. Gyroradius 478 km; mirror latitude 29.8 degrees; bounce period 2.29 s against the textbook estimate 4 L R_E/v (1.30 - 0.56 sin alpha) = 2.39 s; drift 1.22 min, westward, as it must be for protons. |v| conserved to 1e-14. RK4 with the same step loses 98 percent of the energy in those 75 s (5 percent at half the step, 0.17 percent at a quarter): the gyration is fastest near the mirror points, where the field is strongest.

Three timescales, one integrator

  • The step is set by the fastest motion, the gyration. The physics of interest (the drift that forms the belt) is 500 times slower.
  • Over the drift, Boris conserves the energy to $10^{-14}$. RK4, with the same step, loses 98% of it.
  • This is why plasma codes push every particle with Boris.

Structure preservation matters most when the interesting timescale is far longer than the step.

Part II · How NASA integrates orbits

Choosing an integrator in 1963

NASA's comparison, 1963

NASA Space Flight Handbook, vol. 1 (1963), Chapter IV.C: the methods of the last five lectures, rated for computing trajectories.

Runge-Kutta: easy to start, slow. Multistep: fast.

"Unconditionally stable" here means no growing parasitic roots at small $h$, not the stiff stability of Lecture 10.

Their pick for orbits: Gauss-Jackson.

In 1963 usage, "stable" meant that the parasitic roots of a multistep method (Lecture 10) stay inside the unit circle as h goes to zero. Milne's method is the classic failure: one of its parasitic roots sits on the unit circle at h = 0 and moves outside for any small h lambda < 0, so it is rated "unstable". Adams-Moulton's parasitic roots start at 0, hence "unconditionally stable" in that sense; for stiff problems AM4 is still limited to h lambda >= -3.

Gauss-Jackson

  • A multistep predictor-corrector method (Lecture 10), built for second-order equations $\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{r})$: it integrates twice without converting to a first-order system.
  • Eighth order, fixed step, and about one or two evaluations of the force per step.
  • Needs a start-up (a Runge-Kutta run for the first points), like every multistep method.
  • Still used for satellite orbit propagation (Berry & Healy 2004).
The "Gauss-Jackson" name refers to the summed (second-sum) form of the Stormer-Cowell multistep method, which accumulates the accelerations in running sums; that form controls the growth of rounding error over very long runs, the last column of NASA's table. Berry & Healy 2004, J. Astronaut. Sci. 52, 331, is the standard implementation description; the Python port used here follows it.

100 orbits, measured

A circular orbit 7000 km from Earth's center (period 97 minutes), error after 100 orbits:

RK4, 20k evaluations1900 km
DOP853, 40k1.2 m
DOP853, 97k0.7 mm
Gauss-Jackson 8, 20k0.5 mm

High order, one or two evaluations per step, and a smooth periodic problem: the best case for a fixed-step multistep method.

GJ8 at dt = 60 s: 19853 evaluations (2.04 per step: one corrector iteration per step); at dt = 30 s the corrector converges in one evaluation (1.03 per step) and the error is 7e-3 mm. DOP853 is scipy's adaptive 8th-order Runge-Kutta, rtol 1e-10 (40k evaluations, 1.2 m) and 1e-13 (97k, 0.7 mm). So against the best Runge-Kutta the advantage is a factor of about 5 in cost; against RK4 it is overwhelming. Strongly eccentric orbits are harder for any fixed step; that is where adaptive methods (or a change of time variable) come back.

Part III · Molecular dynamics

Newton's equations for many atoms

Molecular dynamics

$N$ atoms, Newton's second law, and a pair potential. For noble-gas atoms, Lennard-Jones:

$$m\,\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i = -\sum_{j\ne i}\nabla_i V(r_{ij}), \qquad V(r) = 4\varepsilon\left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6}\right]$$
  • Integrated with velocity Verlet (Lecture 10): one force evaluation per step, and bounded energy error over millions of steps.
  • The cost is in the forces: all pairs is $O(N^2)$. Cutting the potential off at a few $\sigma$ and keeping neighbor lists makes it $O(N)$.
  • Used for liquids, materials, proteins and drug binding.
The demo uses reduced units (sigma = epsilon = m = 1) in 2D with periodic boundaries and a cutoff at 2.5 sigma, all-pairs forces in numpy. Setup and every number are in make_figures.py, Part III.

Cooling into a crystal

  • 256 atoms, cooled slowly by rescaling all velocities toward a falling target temperature.
  • The jiggling dies down and the atoms line up into a triangular lattice: only Newton's equations and the Lennard-Jones force.
Velocity Verlet, dt = 0.005, density 0.9, kT from 2.5 to 0.05 over 12000 steps, then held for 1500; a frame every 20 steps, 22 s at 30 fps. Colour is each atom's kinetic energy. psi_6 is the global bond-orientational order parameter, |mean of exp(6 i theta)| over neighbor bonds: 0.08 in the hot fluid, 0.94 at the end; it first passes 0.5 at kT = 1.72. Velocity rescaling is a crude thermostat; production codes use gentler ones (Nose-Hoover, Langevin). Triangular packing is the densest arrangement of disks in 2D.

A nano-meteorite hits a crystal

  • A shock wave runs through the lattice at the speed of sound, atoms splash out, and a crater with raised rims is left behind.
  • No thermostat: 12,000 Verlet steps keep the total energy constant to $5 \times 10^{-7}$.
A 19-atom hexagonal cluster at speed 16 (kinetic energy 2432 in units of epsilon) into a 4000-atom triangular crystal, periodic sideways, bottom two rows held fixed; the shock reflects off them. dt = 0.001 because of the fast atoms; 12 time units, a frame every 20 steps, 20 s at 30 fps. 120 atoms end up above the surface. Forces from a neighbor search (scipy cKDTree) instead of all pairs: the O(N) trick from the previous slide, which is what makes 4000 atoms take 90 s in Python. At 5 times the projectile energy the whole slab melts.

Part IV · Cosmology

The universe as an ODE problem

The microwave background

  • Temperature fluctuations of about $10^{-5}$, left from when the universe became transparent, 380,000 years after the big bang. The multipole $\ell$ is an angle on the sky: about $180°/\ell$.
  • The peaks are sound waves in the early plasma. Their positions and heights measure the contents of the universe.
Computed with CAMB (pip install camb) for Planck-like parameters: H0 = 67.4, Omega_b h^2 = 0.0224, Omega_c h^2 = 0.120, n_s = 0.965, tau = 0.054. 0.6 s for the spectrum to l = 2500. Peaks at l = 220, 536, 813, 1126. Recombination at z = 1090; sound horizon 144.4 Mpc; angular size 100 theta_* = 1.0412.

Why it is an ODE problem

  • On large scales the universe is homogeneous: the background (expansion, density, temperature) depends on time alone.
  • The fluctuations are small, so their equations are linear. Fourier transform in space: each wavenumber $k$ evolves on its own.
  • For each $k$, every unknown is a function of one variable:
conformal time $\tau$, with $d\tau = dt / a(t)$. A PDE in space and time becomes a set of ODEs in $\tau$, one set per $k$.
a(t) is the scale factor. The spatial dependence of each perturbation is exp(i k.x) and drops out of the linear equations; isotropy means only |k| matters. The same idea, a linear PDE on a homogeneous background reduced to ODEs mode by mode, returns when we get to PDEs.

The ODEs, for each $k$

Background first (the expansion $a(\tau)$, and the free-electron fraction as hydrogen forms). Then, for each $k$, ODEs for every component:

componentunknownswhat drives them
photonstemperature fluctuation $\Theta = \delta T/T$ in each direction, as angular moments: $\Theta_0$ (temperature), $\Theta_1$ (flow), $\Theta_2, \dots$ gravity, pressure, scattering off electrons
ordinary matterdensity, velocity gravity, pressure, drag from the photons
dark matterdensity, velocity gravity only
neutrinosangular moments, like the photons gravity only: they stream freely
spacetimetwo potentials $\phi, \psi$ Einstein's equations, sourced by all of the above

Stiff early on: photons scatter off electrons far faster than the universe expands.

Conformal Newtonian gauge, Ma & Bertschinger 1995 (ApJ 455, 7). The photon moments are the natural unknowns: the fluid variables used for matter are the first two of them (energy density goes as T^4, so the photon density contrast is 4 Theta_0; Theta_1 is the velocity). The moments form a chain, each coupled to its neighbors, truncated after a few dozen. The simplest pair of equations, dark matter (' = d/d tau, delta the density contrast, theta the velocity divergence): delta_c' = -theta_c + 3 phi', theta_c' = -(a'/a) theta_c + k^2 psi. Baryons add pressure and the photon drag, (4 rho_gamma / 3 rho_b) a n_e sigma_T (theta_gamma - theta_b). A full system is a few dozen to a few hundred ODEs per k, for a few thousand k. Stiffness: codes use a tight-coupling approximation, or an implicit solver (from memory: CLASS defaults to ndf15, a BDF-type method; CAMB uses an explicit Runge-Kutta method with tight coupling; worth checking before quoting).

From the ODEs to $C_\ell$

  • Expand the temperature map of the sky in spherical harmonics: $$\frac{\delta T}{T}(\hat n) = \sum_{\ell m} a_{\ell m}\, Y_{\ell m}(\hat n), \qquad C_\ell = \langle |a_{\ell m}|^2 \rangle$$ $C_\ell$ measures how much the temperature varies on angular scales of about $180°/\ell$.
  • For one wave $k$, the photon moments at our position today, $\Theta_\ell(k, \tau_0)$, are that wave's pattern on our sky.
  • The sky is all the waves together, with random amplitudes from the early universe (spectrum $P(k)$): $$C_\ell = \frac{2}{\pi}\int dk\, k^2\, P(k)\, \big|\Theta_\ell(k, \tau_0)\big|^2$$
Normalization as in Dodelson & Schmidt, with Theta_l per unit initial amplitude. Why a few dozen moments suffice when the plot goes to l = 2500: after recombination the photons stream freely to us, and a wave of size 1/k seen from distance D appears at l of about k D (D = r_s / theta_* = about 13,900 Mpc), so free streaming moves power from low to high l. Following that with the chain of moments would take about 2500 ODEs per k. Instead, codes evolve a few dozen moments through recombination and do the free streaming exactly with a line-of-sight integral over spherical Bessel functions (Seljak & Zaldarriaga 1996), which made CMB codes about 100 times faster.

The solution meets the sky

  • Six parameters, and the ODE solution runs through every Planck point: $\chi^2 = 80$ for 83 binned measurements.
  • This agreement is among the strongest evidence for $\Lambda$CDM: remove dark matter, keeping the same total matter, and the peaks come out wrong.
How k becomes l: a wave of wavenumber k, seen at the distance of last scattering D_* = r_s / theta_* = 144.4 / 0.010412 = about 13,900 Mpc, appears on the sky at l of about k D_*. Data: Planck 2018 (PR3) TT spectrum from the Planck Legacy Archive, binned for l >= 30 and every l below; make_figures.py downloads it each run and does not commit it. The LCDM curve uses rounded Planck parameters (H0 = 67.4, Omega_b h^2 = 0.0224, Omega_c h^2 = 0.120, n_s = 0.965, tau = 0.054); Planck's own best fit gives chi^2 = 65 on the same points. The no-dark-matter curve puts all the matter in baryons (Omega_b h^2 = 0.1424) and is computed without lensing (CAMB cannot compute the lensing for it); its first peak runs off the top of the plot.
