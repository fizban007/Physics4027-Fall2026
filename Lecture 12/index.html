Two ways to pin down a second-order ODE

A second-order ODE, $y'' = f(x, y, y')$, needs two conditions.

  • Initial value problem. Both at the same point: $y(a)$ and $y'(a)$. Everything so far.
  • Boundary value problem. One at each end: $y(a) = \alpha$ and $y(b) = \beta$.
    • A ball thrown from the hand that has to go through the hoop.
    • The electric potential between two electrodes held at fixed voltages.
    • A quantum particle in a box: $\psi = 0$ at both walls.

An IVP can be marched forward one step at a time. A BVP cannot: a naive marching cannot guarantee the solution will meet $y(b) = \beta$.

Part I · Shooting

Find the missing initial condition

Shooting a basket ball

How do you find out the way to shoot a projectile, so that it lands at a target location?

The missing initial condition

$y'' = f(x, y, y')$ with $y(a) = \alpha$ and $y(b) = \beta$. To march it like an IVP, we need $y(a)$ and $y'(a)$. We have only the first.

Shooting: find the initial slope $y'(a)$ whose solution lands on $y(b) = \beta$.

Shooting a basketball

The ball leaves the hand at $10$ m/s, with air drag. The hoop is $6.75$ m away and $1.05$ m higher.

The starting point and the speed are known. The missing initial condition is the angle.

Try an angle, follow the ball to the hoop, see by how much it misses, and adjust.

Two angles go in: a flat shot and a high arc.

What we are solving

Call the missing slope $s$, and solve the IVP

$$y'' = f(x, y, y'), \qquad y(a) = \alpha, \qquad y'(a) = s.$$

The solution $y(x;\, s)$ depends on $s$, and so does where it ends up at $x = b$. Measure the miss:

$$F(s) = y(b;\, s) - \beta$$

Shooting is solving $F(s) = 0$: one equation for one unknown.

One evaluation of $F(s)$:

  1. set $y(a) = \alpha$ and $y'(a) = s$;
  2. integrate to $x = b$ with an IVP solver (Homework 5's Dormand-Prince, landing on $b$ exactly);
  3. return $y(b) - \beta$.

For the basketball, $s$ is the launch angle and $F$ is how far above or below the hoop the ball passes.

Solving $F(s) = 0$

$F$ has no formula, but we can evaluate it at any $s$. Any root finder from Lecture 3 works. The secant method needs no derivative:

$$s_{n+1} = s_n - F(s_n)\,\frac{s_n - s_{n-1}}{F(s_n) - F(s_{n-1})}$$

Each iteration is one new shot. Starting from $30°$ and $35°$: $35.3°$ after 6 shots. Starting from $60°$ and $65°$: $61.6°$ after 9.

$F$ has two roots, so the problem has two solutions. The starting guesses decide which one you get.

DERIVATION (linear ODE): if the ODE is linear, y(x; s) = u(x) + s v(x), where u solves the IVP with u(a) = alpha, u'(a) = 0, and v solves the homogeneous equation with v(a) = 0, v'(a) = 1. So F(s) = u(b) + s v(b) - beta is a straight line in s, and a single secant step from any two shots lands on the root. Bisection also works once two shots bracket a root.

Eigenvalue problems

The time-independent Schrödinger equation for a particle of mass $m$ in a potential $V(x)$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\,\psi = E\,\psi$$
  • The infinite well: $V = 0$ for $0 < x < L$, and $V = \infty$ outside. The particle cannot leave the box, so $\psi$ must vanish at the walls.
  • In units with $\hbar = m = L = 1$: $$-\frac{1}{2}\psi'' = E\psi, \qquad \psi(0) = 0, \quad \psi(1) = 0$$

What to shoot with

At $x = 0$ we know $\psi(0) = 0$. Two things are unknown: the slope $\psi'(0)$ and the energy $E$.

The slope only sets the size: doubling $\psi'(0)$ doubles $\psi$ everywhere, and the zeros stay where they are. So fix $\psi'(0) = 1$.

The energy changes the shape. Too low, and $\psi$ has not come back to zero by $x = 1$; too high, and it crosses zero too early.

So we fix $\psi'(0) = 1$ and shoot in $E$.

Root finding in $E$

Same recipe, with $E$ in place of $s$:

$$F(E) = \psi(1;\, E) = 0$$

Each root is an energy level. The secant method started near the first one finds $E_1 = 4.9348022005$, equal to $\pi^2/2$ to 10 digits, in 9 IVP solves.

Last step: rescale $\psi$ so that $\int_0^1 \psi^2\,dx = 1$.

When shooting fails

A linear BVP with a known exact solution:

$$\phi'' = \kappa^2\phi, \quad \phi(0) = \phi(1) = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \phi(x) = \frac{\cosh\kappa(x - \tfrac{1}{2})}{\cosh(\kappa/2)}$$

Every solution is a mix of $e^{-\kappa x}$ and $e^{+\kappa x}$. The exact one decays away from each end over a length $1/\kappa$, down to $e^{-\kappa/2} \approx 10^{-11}$ in the middle for $\kappa = 50$.

Shooting from $x = 0$, an error $\delta s$ in the slope adds $\frac{\delta s}{\kappa}\sinh(\kappa x)$: a little of $e^{+\kappa x}$, which grows by $e^{\kappa} \approx 5 \times 10^{21}$ on the way across.

Here the slope is right to 15 digits, and the solution still misses by a factor of $10^6$.

Shooting marches across the whole domain. We need a method that does not march.

PHYSICS (to introduce in words): Debye screening between two charged plates. Poisson with Boltzmann charges, eps0 phi'' = -rho; linearized for e phi << kT this is phi'' = phi / lambda_D^2, lambda_D^2 = eps0 kT / (n0 e^2), and with lengths in units of the gap L, kappa = L / lambda_D. Cleanest setting: an electrolyte (salt water) between plates (Debye-Hueckel). For a plasma with fixed ions, same linear equation; but a real plasma at a biased wall forms a sheath, so avoid presenting it as a plasma. DERIVATION: phi'' = kappa^2 phi has solutions e^{-kappa x} and e^{+kappa x}. An error delta_s in the initial slope adds delta_s sinh(kappa x)/kappa to phi(x), about delta_s e^kappa / (2 kappa) at x = 1. With delta_s ~ 1e-16 kappa (rounding of s), that is 1e-16 e^kappa / 2: 2.6e5 at kappa = 50, and above 1 once kappa > 37. Integration errors are amplified the same way. Numbers from make_figures.py: slope found -49.999999999999986, exact -50 (to 15 digits). Bound states in quantum mechanics have the same problem in the walls; the usual fix is to shoot from both ends and match in the middle.

Part II · The grid method

Solve for every point at once

The idea

  • Put a grid on $[a, b]$: $x_i = a + i h$, $i = 0, \dots, N$, with $h = (b - a)/N$. The unknowns are all the values $y_i$ at once.
  • Replace every derivative in the ODE by a difference of neighbouring $y_i$. Each interior point gives one equation.
  • The boundary conditions are simply $y_0 = \alpha$ and $y_N = \beta$. Both ends are imposed exactly, and nothing is integrated from one end to the other.
  • The ODE becomes $N - 1$ algebraic equations for $N - 1$ unknowns. Numerical Recipes calls this the relaxation method; it is also called the finite-difference method.

Finite differences

Taylor-expand the neighbours, $y_{i \pm 1} = y_i \pm h y_i' + \tfrac{h^2}{2} y_i'' \pm \tfrac{h^3}{6} y_i''' + \dots$, and combine:

forward $y_i' \approx \dfrac{y_{i+1} - y_i}{h}$ error $-\tfrac{h}{2} y''$first order
central $y_i' \approx \dfrac{y_{i+1} - y_{i-1}}{2h}$ error $-\tfrac{h^2}{6} y'''$second order
second derivative $y_i'' \approx \dfrac{y_{i+1} - 2 y_i + y_{i-1}}{h^2}$ error $-\tfrac{h^2}{12} y''''$second order

Symmetric formulas cancel the even (or odd) terms of the expansion and gain an order. More neighbours give higher order.

DERIVATION: forward: (y_{i+1} - y_i)/h = y' + h y''/2 + ... Central: y_{i+1} - y_{i-1} = 2 h y' + h^3 y'''/3 + ..., so dividing by 2h leaves y' + h^2 y'''/6. Second derivative: y_{i+1} + y_{i-1} = 2 y_i + h^2 y'' + h^4 y''''/12 + ..., so (y_{i+1} - 2 y_i + y_{i-1})/h^2 = y'' + h^2 y''''/12 + ... The error column is (exact - formula).

The order, and the rounding floor

The derivative of $\sin x$ at $x = 1$. Truncation error falls with slope 1 and 2.

Below $h \approx 10^{-8}$ (forward) and $10^{-5}$ (central), the subtraction $y_{i+1} - y_i$ cancels most digits, and the rounding error grows like $\epsilon/h$: catastrophic cancellation, Lecture 1.

A BVP grid has $h \sim 10^{-3}$ or larger, far from the floor. There, the order is what matters.

The same problem on a grid

At each interior point, $\dfrac{\phi_{i+1} - 2\phi_i + \phi_{i-1}}{h^2} - \kappa^2 \phi_i = 0$. The known $\phi_0$ and $\phi_N$ move to the right-hand side:

$$ \frac{1}{h^2} \begin{pmatrix} -2 - \kappa^2 h^2 & 1 & & \\ 1 & -2 - \kappa^2 h^2 & \ddots & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & 1 \\ & & 1 & -2 - \kappa^2 h^2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \phi_1 \\ \phi_2 \\ \vdots \\ \phi_{N-1} \end{pmatrix} = -\frac{1}{h^2} \begin{pmatrix} \phi_0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ \phi_N \end{pmatrix} $$

A linear ODE becomes one linear system, $\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\phi} = \mathbf{b}$. Each row involves only a point and its two neighbours, so $\mathbf{A}$ is tridiagonal.

Solve it once, and every $\phi_i$ comes out together. No guessing, no iteration.

The grid succeeds where shooting failed

Slope 2 for every $\kappa$, as the second-order formula promises.

At $\kappa = 50$ with $N = 1024$: relative error $2.5 \times 10^{-3}$, where shooting was off by a factor of $10^6$.

Larger $\kappa$ needs a finer grid, since $h$ has to resolve the decay length $1/\kappa$. But the error is controlled by $h$, and no longer amplified by $e^{\kappa}$.

A nonlinear version

Make the same problem nonlinear, $\phi'' = \kappa^2 \sinh\phi$, and use the same grid. One equation per interior point:

$$\frac{\phi_{i+1} - 2\phi_i + \phi_{i-1}}{h^2} - \kappa^2 \sinh\phi_i = 0, \qquad i = 1, \dots, N - 1$$
  • Still $N - 1$ equations for $N - 1$ unknowns, and each one involves only a point and its two neighbours.
  • But $\sinh\phi_i$ is not linear in $\phi_i$, so this is no longer a matrix equation $\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\phi} = \mathbf{b}$ that we can solve in one go.
  • Call the left-hand side $F_i(\boldsymbol{\phi})$. We need $\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\phi}) = 0$: root finding again, now for a vector of unknowns.

Newton's method for many unknowns

one unknown (Lecture 3)a vector of unknowns
linearize $F(x + \delta) \approx F(x) + F'(x)\,\delta$ $\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\phi} + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \approx \mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\phi}) + \mathbf{J}\,\boldsymbol{\delta}$
step $\delta = -F / F'$ solve $\mathbf{J}\,\boldsymbol{\delta} = -\mathbf{F}$
update $x \leftarrow x + \delta$ $\boldsymbol{\phi} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\phi} + \boldsymbol{\delta}$
  • $\mathbf{J}$ is the Jacobian, the matrix of derivatives $J_{ij} = \partial F_i / \partial \phi_j$. It plays the role of $F'$.
  • $F_i$ depends only on $\phi_{i-1}$, $\phi_i$, $\phi_{i+1}$, so $\mathbf{J}$ is tridiagonal, just like the matrix of the linear problem: $1/h^2$ next to the diagonal, $-2/h^2 - \kappa^2\cosh\phi_i$ on it.

Each Newton iteration is one tridiagonal linear system. A nonlinear BVP becomes a sequence of linear ones.

PHYSICS (to introduce in words): the full Poisson-Boltzmann equation for a symmetric electrolyte (two ion species, both Boltzmann): rho = -2 n0 e sinh(e phi / kT), so phi'' = kappa^2 sinh(phi) with phi in units of kT/e. The linear equation is its small-phi limit; phi = 5 is far outside it. DERIVATION: F_i depends on phi_{i-1}, phi_i, phi_{i+1} only. dF_i/dphi_{i+-1} = 1/h^2, dF_i/dphi_i = -2/h^2 - kappa^2 cosh(phi_i), and all other entries are zero. For a general y'' = f(x, y, y') with central differences, the off-diagonal entries pick up -+ (df/dy') / (2h) as well; still tridiagonal.

Shooting or grid?

shootinggrid
reusesyour IVP solver and a root finderfinite differences and a linear solver
accuracyadaptive, set by the toleranceset by $h$: second order here
weak spotgrowing solutions amplify errors across the domainneeds a good initial guess when nonlinear
many unknown functions a multidimensional root finda larger, still sparse, system

Shooting is quick to write when the problem is benign. The grid method is the one that carries over to partial differential equations.

Every grid method ends in $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$

In 2D, each point couples to four neighbours. A $6 \times 6$ grid gives a $36 \times 36$ matrix with 156 nonzeros out of 1296.

A $1000 \times 1000$ grid has $10^6$ unknowns. How do we solve that system, and how fast?

The square well on a grid becomes $\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\psi} = E\boldsymbol{\psi}$: a matrix eigenvalue problem.
