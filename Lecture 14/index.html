Last time

  • Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting gives $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{U}$.
  • Factoring costs about $\tfrac{2}{3}n^3$; each solve with $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ afterwards about $n^2$.
  • That does not scale: the $10^6$ unknowns of a 2D grid would take about 9 years.

Today: matrices from physics are rarely arbitrary. Their structure makes them far cheaper to solve.

Part I · Tridiagonal systems

Only three diagonals

Tridiagonal matrices

$$ \begin{pmatrix} b_0 & c_0 & & & \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 & & \\ & a_2 & b_2 & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & \ddots & c_{n-2} \\ & & & a_{n-1} & b_{n-1} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_0 \\ x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_{n-1} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} r_0 \\ r_1 \\ r_2 \\ \vdots \\ r_{n-1} \end{pmatrix} $$
  • Store three vectors $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{c}$ instead of the whole matrix: $3n$ numbers instead of $n^2$.
  • Every point coupled only to its two neighbours: the BVP grid of Lecture 12 ($b_i = -2 - \kappa^2 h^2$, $a_i = c_i = 1$), cubic splines, implicit methods for diffusion.

Elimination stays inside the band

  • Below each pivot $u_{i-1}$ there is only one nonzero entry, $a_i$. So each column needs one multiplier, $l_i$, and changes only the next row.
  • That row's super-diagonal $c_i$ is untouched, since the entry above it in row $i-1$ is zero. The factors are
$$ \mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & & & \\ l_1 & 1 & & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \\ & & l_{n-1} & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_0 & c_0 & & \\ & u_1 & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & c_{n-2} \\ & & & u_{n-1} \end{pmatrix} $$

$\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ are as sparse as $\mathbf{A}$. Nothing fills in.

The recipe (Thomas algorithm)

Start with $u_0 = b_0$, $y_0 = r_0$. Then, top to bottom, for $i = 1, \dots, n-1$:

$$l_i = \frac{a_i}{u_{i-1}}, \qquad u_i = b_i - l_i\,c_{i-1}, \qquad y_i = r_i - l_i\,y_{i-1}$$

Then back substitution, bottom to top:

$$x_{n-1} = \frac{y_{n-1}}{u_{n-1}}, \qquad x_i = \frac{y_i - c_i\,x_{i+1}}{u_i}, \quad i = n-2, \dots, 0$$

The first loop is elimination and forward substitution together; the second is back substitution. About $8n$ operations in all, and about ten lines of C++.

The ten-line C++ version is solve_tridiagonal in tridiagonal_timing.cpp, in the course's C++ subset. DERIVATION: matching (LU)_{i,i-1} = l_i u_{i-1} = a_i and (LU)_{ii} = l_i c_{i-1} + u_i = b_i gives the first two formulas; (LU)_{i,i+1} = c_i holds automatically. Count: per row, l_i is 1 division, u_i 2 operations, y_i 2, x_i 3.

From years to milliseconds

The same matrix, solved two ways, with our own C++ code.

Dense LU at $n = 10^6$: about 9 years (extrapolated).

Tridiagonal at $n = 10^6$: 10 ms. At $10^7$: 86 ms.

The time grows like $n$: twice the unknowns, twice the time.

What about pivoting?

  • The recipe never swaps rows. A swap would push entries outside the three diagonals.
  • It is safe when the matrix is diagonally dominant: each diagonal entry at least as large as the rest of its row, $$|b_i| \ge |a_i| + |c_i|,$$ with $>$ in at least one row. Then no $u_i$ can become small.
  • Matrices from grids usually are. Lecture 12: $|{-2} - \kappa^2 h^2| \ge 1 + 1$, with $>$ in the first and last rows.
  • If not, use a banded LU with pivoting (LAPACK's gbsv, behind solve_banded).
Precisely: strict diagonal dominance in every row, or weak dominance with strict in at least one row for an irreducible matrix (every point connected to every other through the couplings), guarantees nonsingularity and that elimination without pivoting is stable. The Lecture 12 matrices are irreducible.

Banded matrices

  • A matrix with $p$ nonzero diagonals on each side of the main one. Elimination again stays inside the band: about $n p^2$ operations, storage $n p$.
  • A 2D grid of $N \times N$ points, numbered row by row: the neighbours above and below are $N$ places away in the numbering, so $p = N$.
  • $N = 1000$: $n = 10^6$ unknowns, about $n p^2 = 10^{12}$ operations. Of order ten minutes, instead of 9 years.

Structure turned years into minutes. Sparse direct solvers and iterative methods go further still.

Inside the band elimination does fill in: the band of width N becomes dense, n p = 1e9 numbers, 8 GB. Our dense code ran at about 2.4e9 operations per second (2n^3/3 at n = 3200 in 9 s), so 2 n p^2 = 2e12 operations is about 14 minutes.

Part II · Cholesky decomposition

Symmetric positive definite matrices

Symmetric positive definite

  • Symmetric: $a_{ij} = a_{ji}$, so $\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{A}$.
  • Positive definite: $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} > 0$ for every $\mathbf{v} \neq 0$. Equivalently, all eigenvalues are positive.

Physics is full of them, because they are energies:

  • Coupled oscillators: the potential energy $\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x} > 0$ for any displacement, so the spring matrix $\mathbf{K}$ is positive definite.
  • Covariance matrices of noise or of fitted parameters; $\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}$ in least squares (Lecture 16).

$\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T$: a 2 × 2 example

For symmetric positive definite $\mathbf{A}$, look for $\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{L}^T$:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} l_{11} & 0 \\ l_{21} & l_{22} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} l_{11} & l_{21} \\ 0 & l_{22} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} l_{11}^2 & l_{11}l_{21} \\ l_{11}l_{21} & l_{21}^2 + l_{22}^2 \end{pmatrix} $$

Match the entries one at a time:

$$l_{11} = \sqrt{4} = 2, \qquad l_{21} = \frac{2}{l_{11}} = 1, \qquad l_{22} = \sqrt{5 - l_{21}^2} = 2$$

So $\mathbf{L} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$: a lower-triangular "square root" of $\mathbf{A}$.

The recipe

The same matching, column by column, $i = 1, \dots, n$:

$$ \begin{aligned} l_{ii} &= \Big(a_{ii} - \sum_{k < i} l_{ik}^2\Big)^{1/2} \\ l_{ji} &= \frac{1}{l_{ii}}\Big(a_{ji} - \sum_{k < i} l_{jk}\,l_{ik}\Big), \qquad j > i \end{aligned} $$

Then solve $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ as with LU: forward substitution with $\mathbf{L}$, back substitution with $\mathbf{L}^T$.

DERIVATION: (L L^T)_{ji} = sum_{k <= i} l_{jk} l_{ik} for j >= i. Setting j = i gives the diagonal formula; j > i the column below. Each l_{ji} needs only entries from earlier columns, so the recipe can run column by column.

Why use Cholesky

  • Half the work of LU, about $\tfrac{1}{3}n^3$, and half the storage: only $\mathbf{L}$ is kept.
  • No pivoting needed. It is stable for every symmetric positive definite matrix.
  • A free test. If $\mathbf{A}$ is not positive definite, a square root of a negative number appears. Attempting Cholesky is the cheapest way to check.
Stability without pivoting: each l_{ji}^2 <= a_{jj} (from the diagonal formula for row j), so the entries of L can never grow larger than the square roots of the diagonal of A.

Tell the library what you know

  • numpy.linalg.solve always uses general LU with partial pivoting (LAPACK's gesv).
  • scipy.linalg.solve(A, b, assume_a="pos") uses Cholesky, for symmetric positive definite $\mathbf{A}$. scipy.linalg.solve_banded handles tridiagonal and banded matrices.
  • Eigen (C++): PartialPivLU for general matrices, LLT for Cholesky, and a separate module for sparse matrices.

The library cannot guess the structure. If you do not tell it, it pays the full $n^3$.

Part III · QR decomposition

Orthogonal times triangular

$\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}$

  • $\mathbf{R}$ is upper triangular. $\mathbf{Q}$ is orthogonal: its columns are unit vectors, perpendicular to each other, so $\mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}$.
  • For the matrix we will use below:
$$ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} -0.873 & -0.195 & -0.447 \\ -0.436 & -0.098 & 0.894 \\ -0.218 & 0.976 & 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{Q}} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} -4.583 & -2.182 & -1.528 \\ 0 & -0.488 & 2.733 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.447 \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{R}} $$

The first column of $\mathbf{Q}$ is the first column of $\mathbf{A}$, normalized: $-(4, 2, 1)/\sqrt{21}$. How to compute $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{R}$ in general: next lecture.

Why orthogonal matrices are safe

  • The inverse is free: $\mathbf{Q}^{-1} = \mathbf{Q}^T$.
  • Lengths are preserved: $|\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}| = |\mathbf{x}|$. A rotation or reflection does not stretch anything, so it cannot amplify rounding errors.
  • Solving: $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ becomes $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{b}$, then back substitution.
  • The price: about twice the work of LU. For square systems LU is the default. QR is the tool for rectangular matrices (least squares, Lecture 16) and for eigenvalues.
DERIVATION: |Q x|^2 = x^T Q^T Q x = x^T x. Cost: Householder QR of a square matrix is about 4n^3/3 operations, against 2n^3/3 for LU.

Eigenvalues from QR

Start with $\mathbf{A}_0 = \mathbf{A}$, and repeat:

$$\text{factor } \mathbf{A}_k = \mathbf{Q}_k\mathbf{R}_k, \qquad \text{multiply in reverse: } \mathbf{A}_{k+1} = \mathbf{R}_k\mathbf{Q}_k$$
  • Since $\mathbf{R}_k = \mathbf{Q}_k^T\mathbf{A}_k$: $\mathbf{A}_{k+1} = \mathbf{Q}_k^T\mathbf{A}_k\mathbf{Q}_k$. This is a change of basis, so $\mathbf{A}_{k+1}$ has the same eigenvalues as $\mathbf{A}_k$, and so as $\mathbf{A}$.
  • The iterates become triangular (diagonal, if $\mathbf{A}$ is symmetric). The eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal entries.
DERIVATION (same eigenvalues): if A_k v = lambda v, then A_{k+1} (Q_k^T v) = Q_k^T A_k Q_k Q_k^T v = Q_k^T A_k v = lambda (Q_k^T v). Why it converges: the product Q_0 Q_1 ... Q_k is the orthogonalized result of k steps of power iteration on all columns at once; the (i, j) off-diagonal entry shrinks like |lambda_j / lambda_i|^k for |lambda_i| > |lambda_j|. A non-symmetric matrix converges to upper triangular (Schur) form.

The QR iteration on our matrix

  • The diagonal settles on the eigenvalues $5.346$, $2.722$, $-0.069$ within a few iterations.
  • The off-diagonal entries shrink by about a factor of 2 per iteration, the ratio $2.72/5.35$ of two eigenvalues: $10^{-3}$ after 10 iterations, $10^{-9}$ after 30. Close eigenvalues would mean slow convergence.

What the libraries actually do

  • First, bring $\mathbf{A}$ to tridiagonal form (if symmetric) with orthogonal transformations. Each QR step then costs only about $n$ operations.
  • Then QR iterations with shifts, which make convergence fast even for close eigenvalues: a few iterations per eigenvalue.
  • This is numpy.linalg.eigh (symmetric) and eig (general): about $n^3$ in total.
  • The particle in a box on a grid is already tridiagonal: scipy.linalg.eigh_tridiagonal skips the first step.
