Part I · The advection equation

A profile carried along at constant speed

What a PDE is

An equation for a function of several variables, involving its partial derivatives. The ones physics runs on:

diffusion$\partial_t u = D\,\partial_x^2 u$heat, particles spreading
wave$\partial_t^2 u = c^2\,\partial_x^2 u$strings, sound, light
Schrödinger$i\hbar\,\partial_t \psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2 \psi + V\psi$quantum mechanics in time
Poisson$\partial_x^2 \phi + \partial_y^2 \phi = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$electrostatics, gravity
Euler$\partial_t \rho + \partial_x(\rho v) = 0$, and two morefluids and gases
  • Same two kinds as for ODEs. Diffusion, wave and Schrödinger are initial value problems: given $u$ at $t = 0$, march forward. Poisson is a boundary value problem: $\phi$ on the boundary, solve for the inside.
  • Same plan as BVP in ODEs: put space on a grid, replace derivatives by differences, and the PDE becomes a large system of ODEs, or a large linear system.
$\partial_t$ is shorthand for $\partial/\partial t$; say so once. The Poisson equation is the one they met on a grid in Lecture 12 and in Homework 7 in one dimension. Euler's equations are the Project 2 target: the first is conservation of mass; momentum and energy are the other two.

The simplest PDE

Something is carried along at a constant speed $v$, without changing shape. Dye in a pipe, a pulse on a conveyor belt, a density of cars on a road where everyone drives at $v$. Its density $u(x, t)$ obeys

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + v\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0 .$$
  • First order in $t$ and in $x$, linear, with one parameter. Called the advection equation: advection is transport by a flow.
  • An initial value problem: give $u(x, 0) = u_0(x)$ and ask for $u(x, t)$ later.

Everything in fluid dynamics has a term of this form in it. If a method cannot do this equation, it cannot do fluids.

DERIVATION of the equation from conservation: the amount of stuff in $[x, x + dx]$ is $u\,dx$; it changes because stuff flows in at $x$ at the rate $v u(x)$ and out at $x + dx$ at the rate $v u(x + dx)$. So $\partial_t (u\,dx) = v u(x) - v u(x + dx) = -v\,\partial_x u\,dx$. The flux is $v u$; for constant $v$ the equation is $\partial_t u + \partial_x (v u) = 0$, the conservation form that Project 2 will use.

Its exact solution

Guess that the profile just moves: $u(x, t) = f(x - vt)$ for some function $f$. Check it with the chain rule, with $f'$ the derivative of $f$ with respect to its argument:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -v\,f'(x - vt), \qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = f'(x - vt), \qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + v\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0 .$$
  • It works for every $f$. The initial condition picks it: $u(x, 0) = f(x)$, so $f = u_0$ and

$u(x, t) = u_0(x - vt)$

  • The profile at time $t$ is the initial profile shifted by $vt$. No spreading, no decay, no change of shape. A kink stays a kink.
Uniqueness is not shown here; the characteristics on the next slide give it, because they determine $u$ at every point from one initial value.

Characteristics

Follow a point that moves with the flow: its position is $X(t) = x_0 + vt$. The value of $u$ seen from it changes at the rate

$\dfrac{d}{dt}\,u\big(X(t), t\big) = \left[\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \dfrac{dX}{dt}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right]_{x = X(t)} = \left[\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t} + v\,\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right]_{x = X(t)} = 0 .$

$u$ is constant along these lines. They are the characteristics of the equation, and the PDE says: nothing happens along them.

The first equality is the chain rule for $u(X(t), t)$, with the partial derivatives evaluated at $x = X(t)$; the second uses $dX/dt = v$; the third is the PDE. Capital $X$ is the moving point, lower-case $x$ the coordinate; the line it traces in the figure is $x = X(t)$. Read the figure: each line carries one value, the colour, from $t = 0$ upward. To find $u(x, t)$, go down the line through $(x, t)$ to $t = 0$ and read $u_0$ there: $u(x, t) = u_0(x - vt)$. Same solution, now with a reason.

What the lines tell you

  • The value at $(x, t)$ depends on the initial data at one point, $x - vt$. Nothing else matters.
  • Information travels at speed $v$, no faster and no slower. A change made at $x_0$ at $t = 0$ is felt at $x$ exactly at $t = (x - x_0)/v$.
  • On a finite domain $[0, L]$ with $v > 0$, the characteristics enter at $x = 0$ and leave at $x = L$. So a boundary condition is needed at the inflow end only. Prescribing $u$ at the outflow end would contradict what the characteristic brings there.
  • Sharp features are preserved. The equation does not smooth anything; a numerical method that does is adding something of its own.

Each of these will come back when we build a scheme: where to take information from, how far it can travel in one step, where boundary conditions belong.

Part II · Characteristics beyond the toy

Four changes to the equation, one idea

Variable speed

Let the speed depend on position: $\partial_t u + v(x)\,\partial_x u = 0$. Follow a point moving with the local flow, $dX/dt = v(X)$. The chain rule, with the partial derivatives taken at the point $x = X(t)$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\,u\big(X(t), t\big) = \left[\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + v\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right]_{x = X(t)} = 0 .$$
  • The bracket is the PDE's left side at the moving point, and the PDE holds everywhere. So $u$ is constant along each curve, exactly as before.
  • Only the curves have changed: each is the solution of the ODE $dX/dt = v(X)$ from its own starting point $x_0$, and it is bent where $v$ varies.

To find $u$ at $(x, t)$: go back down the curve through $(x, t)$ to $t = 0$, and read $u_0$ there. The next slide does this step by step.

The chain rule gives $\partial_t u + (dX/dt)\,\partial_x u$ with both partials at $x = X(t)$; $dX/dt = v(X(t))$ is $v(x)$ at that point, which is why the bracket is the PDE's left side. Two characteristics never cross: through any point of the $(x, t)$ plane there is exactly one solution of $dX/dt = v(X)$, by uniqueness for ODEs (Lecture 6). So $u$ is single-valued for all time.

Building the solution

in generalexample: $v(x) = x$
1. the curve from $x_0$ solve $dX/dt = v(X)$ with $X(0) = x_0$ $dX/dt = X$, so $X(t) = x_0\,e^{t}$
2. the value it carries $u = u_0(x_0)$ all along the curve $u_0(x_0)$
3. which curve reaches $(x, t)$? solve $X(t) = x$ for $x_0$ $x_0\,e^{t} = x$, so $x_0 = x\,e^{-t}$
4. read off $u$ $u(x, t) = u_0(x_0)$ $u(x, t) = u_0(x\,e^{-t})$
  • Check: for constant $v$, step 1 gives $X = x_0 + vt$, step 3 gives $x_0 = x - vt$, and step 4 is $u_0(x - vt)$ again.
  • For $v = x$ the flow stretches: the profile $u_0(x e^{-t})$ is the initial one widened by the factor $e^{t}$. Where $v$ decreases with $x$ it compresses instead.
  • Step 3 is the only hard part. When it cannot be done by hand, run the ODE $dX/dt = v(X)$ backward from $(x, t)$ to $t = 0$ with any solver from Homework 4 or 5.
DERIVATION of the check for $v = x$: $u = u_0(x e^{-t})$ gives $\partial_t u = -x e^{-t} u_0'$ and $x\,\partial_x u = x e^{-t} u_0'$, which cancel. The width grows as $e^{t}$ because the flow is faster the farther out: outer points run away from inner ones. The movie on the next slide uses $v = 1 + \frac{1}{2}\sin x$, where step 3 has no closed form and the curves were integrated numerically.

Converging curves steepen the profile

  • $v(x) = 1 + \frac{1}{2}\sin x$. Where $v$ decreases with $x$ the front of the pulse slows before the back does, the characteristics bunch, and the profile compresses: its steepest slope doubles, from $1.21$ to $2.42$, by $t = 3$.
  • The values are unchanged; only their spacing is. The maximum is still $1$.
Numbers from make_figures.py: the peak moves from $x = 0$ to $x = 3.65$ at $t = 3$, and the maximum slope of the profile goes from $1.21$ to $2.42$. The region where $v' < 0$ is $\pi/2 < x < 3\pi/2$, which is where the pulse is at $t = 3$.

A source term

Let something happen to $u$ along the way, a decay, a chemical reaction, a force: $\partial_t u + v\,\partial_x u = s(x, t, u)$. Along $dX/dt = v$ the same chain rule gives

$$\frac{d}{dt}\,u\big(X(t), t\big) = s\big(X(t), t, u\big) .$$
  • $u$ is no longer constant along the characteristic, but it obeys an ordinary differential equation there, with $X(t)$ known.
  • Example: $s = -u/\tau$. Along each line $u$ decays as $e^{-t/\tau}$, so $u(x, t) = u_0(x - vt)\,e^{-t/\tau}$.

A first-order PDE is a family of ODEs, one along each characteristic. You already know how to solve ODEs.

DERIVATION of the example: along the line, $du/dt = -u/\tau$ with $u = u_0(x_0)$ at $t = 0$, so $u = u_0(x_0) e^{-t/\tau}$, and $x_0 = x - vt$. Check against the PDE: $\partial_t u = -v u_0' e^{-t/\tau} - u/\tau$, $v\,\partial_x u = v u_0' e^{-t/\tau}$; the sum is $-u/\tau$. Figure char_source.png shows it with $\tau = 2$: the peak at $t = 3$ is $e^{-3/2} = 0.223$. This is how transport with reactions is actually solved in some codes: trace characteristics, integrate ODEs along them.

The recipe

For any equation of the form

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + a(x, t, u)\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = s(x, t, u),$$

solve, from every starting point $x_0$ with $u = u_0(x_0)$, the pair of ODEs

$$\frac{dX}{dt} = a(X, t, u), \qquad \frac{du}{dt} = s(X, t, u) .$$
  • The first says where the characteristic goes; the second says what $u$ does along it. They are coupled when $a$ depends on $u$.
  • This is the method of characteristics. It turns one PDE into many ODEs, and it works as long as the characteristics do not cross.
DERIVATION: with $dX/dt = a$, the chain rule gives $du/dt = \partial_t u + a\,\partial_x u = s$ by the PDE. The fully general first-order equation $A\,\partial_t u + B\,\partial_x u = C$ is the same after dividing by $A$, or with a parameter $s$ along the curve: $dt/ds = A$, $dx/ds = B$, $du/ds = C$. Not needed here.

Nonlinear speed: Burgers' equation

Let the speed be the value itself, $a = u$. The equation

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0$$

is the simplest model of a fluid: $u$ is the velocity, and each bit of fluid keeps its velocity and moves with it. The recipe gives $dX/dt = u$ and $du/dt = 0$:

  • $u$ is constant along each characteristic, so each characteristic is a straight line, with slope set by the value it carries: $x = x_0 + u_0(x_0)\,t$.
  • Where $u_0$ is large the lines are fast. Fast parts of the profile overtake slow parts.
This is the inviscid Burgers equation. Traffic flow is the other standard picture, with $u$ replaced by a density-dependent speed. For gases the same mechanism, with sound added, gives shock waves; Project 2 will meet it.

They cross

  • Two neighbouring characteristics from $x_0$ and $x_0 + dx_0$ meet when $x_0 + u_0 t = x_0 + dx_0 + (u_0 + u_0'\,dx_0)\,t$, that is at $t = -1/u_0'(x_0)$.
  • The first crossing is the breaking time, $t_b = -1/\min u_0'$. For $u_0 = e^{-x^2}$, $\min u_0' = -0.858$ at $x_0 = 0.71$, so $t_b = 1.17$.
DERIVATION: the two lines $x = x_0 + u_0(x_0) t$ and $x = x_0 + dx_0 + u_0(x_0 + dx_0) t$ with $u_0(x_0 + dx_0) = u_0 + u_0' dx_0$ coincide when $dx_0 (1 + u_0' t) = 0$, so $t = -1/u_0'$. Only $u_0' < 0$ gives a positive time: crossing happens where the profile decreases with $x$, the front of the pulse. The steepness at $(x, t)$ is $u_0'(x_0)/(1 + u_0'(x_0) t)$, which goes to infinity at $t_b$: that is the vertical tangent in the right panel. $\min u_0'$ for $e^{-x^2}$: $u_0'' = (4x^2 - 2) e^{-x^2} = 0$ at $x = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $u_0' = -\sqrt{2} e^{-1/2} = -0.858$.

After the breaking time

  • The folded profile is not a solution: $u$ must have one value. What forms instead is a shock, a jump, and the characteristics end when they run into it.
  • The jump moves at the average of the two sides, $(u_L + u_R)/2$, which is what keeps the area under $u$ conserved. Here it slows as the pulse behind it drains.

Characteristics say when the smooth solution ends; conservation says what happens next. Gases do this, and Project 2 will have to survive it.

DERIVATION of the shock speed: integrate the conservation law over an interval $[x_1, x_2]$ containing the jump at $x_s(t)$: $\frac{d}{dt}\int u\,dx = F(u_1) - F(u_2)$ with $F = u^2/2$. The left side is $(u_L - u_R)\,\dot x_s$ plus terms that vanish as the interval shrinks. So $\dot x_s = (F_L - F_R)/(u_L - u_R) = (u_L + u_R)/2$: the Rankine-Hugoniot condition. Which conservation law matters: $u$ and $u^2$ both satisfy an equation of this form in the smooth region, but give different shock speeds, so the physics has to say what is conserved. For a gas it is mass, momentum and energy.

Two families: the wave equation

The wave equation is second order, but it factorizes into two first-order advection operators:

$$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} - c^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} + c\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right) \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} - c\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right) u = 0 .$$
  • Anything killed by either factor is a solution: $f(x - ct)$ moves right, $g(x + ct)$ moves left, and the general solution is $u = f(x - ct) + g(x + ct)$ (d'Alembert).
  • Two families of characteristics, $x - ct =$ const and $x + ct =$ const, each carrying its own piece of the solution.
  • A pulse released from rest splits into two halves of height $\tfrac{1}{2}u_0$ moving apart.
DERIVATION of the factorization: expand the product; the cross terms $\pm c\,\partial_t\partial_x$ cancel. Released from rest means $\partial_t u = 0$ at $t = 0$: with $u = f + g$ this gives $-c f' + c g' = 0$, so $f = g$ up to a constant, and $f = g = u_0/2$. That is the right panel of the next figure.

The domain of dependence

  • The value at $(x, t)$ depends only on the initial data in $[x - ct,\ x + ct]$: the two characteristics through the point, traced back.
  • For advection the interval was a single point. For a gas there are three families, at $v$ and $v \pm c_s$, with $c_s$ the sound speed.

Any numerical scheme has a domain of dependence too: the grid points it reads. It had better contain the physical one.

The three families for a gas come from the three Euler equations written in characteristic form; Project 2 will show them. The last line is the CFL condition, stated before any scheme exists; Part III makes it concrete.

Part III · From characteristics to a scheme

Back to constant $v$, and onto a grid

The grid

Points $x_j = j\,\Delta x$ and times $t_n = n\,\Delta t$; the unknowns are $u_j^n \approx u(x_j, t_n)$. Replace the derivatives by the differences of Lecture 12, centered in space and forward in time:

$$\frac{u_j^{n+1} - u_j^n}{\Delta t} + v\,\frac{u_{j+1}^n - u_{j-1}^n}{2\Delta x} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad u_j^{n+1} = u_j^n - \frac{v\Delta t}{2\Delta x}\left(u_{j+1}^n - u_{j-1}^n\right).$$
  • Each new value comes from three old ones. This is Euler's method applied to the system of ODEs $du_j/dt = -v\,(u_{j+1} - u_{j-1})/2\Delta x$, one per grid point. The scheme is called FTCS: forward time, centered space.
  • The one number that matters is the combination $C = v\Delta t/\Delta x$: how many grid spacings the flow moves in one step.

FTCS fails

  • $C = 0.5$, 200 grid points, periodic box. The peak grows to $1.5$ after 240 steps and the box is filled with a grid-scale oscillation of amplitude $2 \times 10^3$ after 400.
  • Smaller $\Delta t$ does not save it; it only delays the blow-up. The scheme is unstable for every $C$. Next lecture shows why, by following a single wave on the grid.
Numbers from make_figures.py. What goes wrong, in one sentence for now: the centered difference does not know which way the wind blows, and takes half its information from downstream, where the solution has not come from. The von Neumann analysis next time gives $|\xi|^2 = 1 + C^2 \sin^2 k\Delta x > 1$ for every mode.

Ask the characteristic

The exact solution says where the new value comes from: go down the characteristic for one step,

$$u(x_j, t_{n+1}) = u(x_j - v\Delta t,\ t_n) .$$
  • The foot of the characteristic, $x_j - v\Delta t$, is not a grid point. Read the old solution there by linear interpolation between the two grid points around it, $x_{j-1}$ and $x_j$.

The upwind scheme

The foot lies a fraction $C = v\Delta t/\Delta x$ of the way from $x_j$ back to $x_{j-1}$. Linear interpolation gives

$$u_j^{n+1} = (1 - C)\,u_j^n + C\,u_{j-1}^n .$$
  • Rearranged, it is a difference scheme: $$\frac{u_j^{n+1} - u_j^n}{\Delta t} + v\,\frac{u_j^n - u_{j-1}^n}{\Delta x} = 0 .$$ The space derivative is one-sided, taken from the side the flow comes from: upwind. For $v < 0$ use $u_{j+1} - u_j$ instead.
  • Same cost as FTCS, and it only ever reads information from where the characteristic says it is.
DERIVATION of the interpolation: the foot is at $x_j - C\Delta x$; linear interpolation between $(x_{j-1}, u_{j-1})$ and $(x_j, u_j)$ at that point gives $u_j + (u_{j-1} - u_j)\,C$. Expanding the rearranged form in Taylor series shows the scheme is first order in both $\Delta t$ and $\Delta x$; next lecture.

When it is exact, and when it breaks

  • $C = 1$: the foot lands exactly on $x_{j-1}$, and the scheme is $u_j^{n+1} = u_{j-1}^n$, a pure shift. The exact solution, to rounding.
  • $C > 1$: the foot lies beyond $x_{j-1}$, outside the two points used. Interpolation has become extrapolation, with a negative weight $1 - C$, and the errors grow every step.
  • $C < 1$: stable, but each step averages two neighbours, and averaging smooths. The profile smears a little every step.

$C = v\Delta t/\Delta x \le 1$ is the CFL condition (Courant, Friedrichs, Lewy, 1928): in one step, the flow may not move farther than the scheme can see. The numerical domain of dependence must contain the physical one.

With $C > 1$ the weights $(1 - C)$ and $C$ no longer lie in $[0, 1]$, so a bounded profile can produce a value outside its own range, and does, every step. The general statement: if the true value at $(x_j, t_{n+1})$ depends on a point the stencil does not include, no choice of weights can be right for every initial condition. That is the CFL argument, and it applies to every explicit scheme, including the ones for the wave equation and for gases, where the fastest characteristic $v + c_s$ sets the limit.

Upwind in action

  • One crossing of the box. At $C = 1$ both profiles return exactly. At $C = 0.5$ the Gaussian peak has fallen to $0.71$ and the step's corners are gone: the averaging of 400 steps.
  • The smearing is the price of stability in this scheme. It looks like diffusion, and next lecture shows it is diffusion, with a coefficient set by $\Delta x$ and $C$.
Numbers from make_figures.py: 200 points, box length 10, 200 steps at $C = 1$ and 400 at $C = 0.5$. Maximum error at $C = 0.5$: $0.29$ for the Gaussian, $0.48$ for the step. Next lecture: the modified equation of upwind has a diffusion term $\frac{1}{2} v \Delta x (1 - C)\,\partial_x^2 u$, zero at $C = 1$.

Summary

  • Advection: $\partial_t u + v\,\partial_x u = 0$ carries the profile along, $u = u_0(x - vt)$.
  • Characteristics: the curves $dX/dt = a$ along which the PDE becomes the ODE $du/dt = s$. Variable speed bends them, a source changes $u$ along them, nonlinearity makes them cross: a shock, at $t_b = -1/\min u_0'$.
  • Domain of dependence: where the value at $(x, t)$ comes from. One point for advection, an interval for waves, three families for a gas.
  • Upwind: read the old solution at the foot of the characteristic. Stable for $C = v\Delta t/\Delta x \le 1$, the CFL condition, exact at $C = 1$, smearing below it.
