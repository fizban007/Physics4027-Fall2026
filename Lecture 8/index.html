Part I · Estimating the error

What one step can learn about itself

Last time

The Runge-Kutta family: estimate a few slopes, step with a weighted average.

$$k_i = f\Big(t_n + c_i h,\;\, y_n + h \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} a_{ij}\, k_j\Big), \qquad y_{n+1} = y_n + h \sum_{i=1}^{s} b_i\, k_i$$
  • In everything so far, $h$ is fixed: chosen beforehand, the same for every step.
  • Chosen how? Accuracy and stability both constrain it — but you have to guess before you have seen the solution.

The problem with a fixed step

$y(t) = \sin\big(e^{-t^2/a^2}\, \omega t\big)$, with $a = 3$, $\omega = 20$

  • What fixed $h$ would you pick for $t \in [-20, 20]$?
  • The $h$ that resolves the burst at $t = 0$ is absurdly small for the flat wings. Whatever you choose is wrong somewhere.
The instantaneous frequency at t = 0 is omega = 20, so the local period is about 0.3; the wings are essentially constant for |t| > 10. A fixed h pays the worst-case price on the entire interval. Goal for today: the step size should be discovered by the integrator, not guessed by us.

Step doubling

To adapt $h$ we first need to know the error of a step. Simplest idea: take the step twice.

$$\begin{align} y^{(1)} &= \mathrm{RK4}(t_n, y_n, h) \\ y^{(2)} &= \mathrm{RK4}\big(t_n + \tfrac{h}{2},\; \mathrm{RK4}(t_n, y_n, \tfrac{h}{2}),\; \tfrac{h}{2}\big) \end{align}$$
  • One full RK4 step of $h$, and two RK4 steps of $h/2$, from the same starting point.
  • The error estimate is the difference: $\varepsilon = |y^{(1)} - y^{(2)}|$.
  • How many evaluations of $f$ does one trial cost?
Evaluation count: 4 + 4 + 4, minus one because k_1 of the full step and k_1 of the first half step are the same evaluation f(t_n, y_n): 11 per attempted step, versus 4 for a plain RK4 step. This factor of ~3 is the price of knowing the error; the embedded methods of Part IV get it down.

How the estimate scales with $h$

RK4 is 4th-order accurate, so one step of size $h$ carries a local error $c\, h^5$:

$$\begin{align} y(t_n + h) &= y^{(1)} + c\, h^5 + O(h^6) \\ y(t_n + h) &= y^{(2)} + 2\, c\, (h/2)^5 + O(h^6) \end{align}$$
  • Subtracting: $\varepsilon = |y^{(1)} - y^{(2)}| = \tfrac{15}{16}\, c\, h^5 \propto h^5$.
  • The half-step result is 16 times better; its true error is roughly $\varepsilon / 15$.
DERIVATION: the two half steps each commit c (h/2)^5 with the same leading coefficient c (f is smooth, so c barely changes across the step), hence the factor 2. Then y^(2) - y^(1) = c h^5 (1 - 2/32) = (15/16) c h^5, and the error left in y^(2) is c h^5 / 16 = epsilon / 15. Advancing with y^(2) plus epsilon/15 is "local extrapolation" (5th order); we keep it simple and just advance with y^(2).

Part II · From error to step size

Choosing $h$ according to estimated error

The control law

Suppose the step just taken gave error $\varepsilon$, and we want the next one to give $\varepsilon_\mathrm{target}$. Since $\varepsilon = C h^5$ with the same $C$ nearby:

$$\frac{\varepsilon_\mathrm{target}}{h'^5} = \frac{\varepsilon}{h^5} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h' = h \left(\frac{\varepsilon_\mathrm{target}} {\varepsilon}\right)^{1/5}$$

The same formula works in both directions: it shrinks $h$ when the step was too coarse, and grows $h$ when the step was wastefully accurate.

Choosing the target error

We know nothing about $y(t)$ in advance, so the standard target combines two tolerances:

$$\varepsilon_\mathrm{target} = \epsilon_\mathrm{abs} + \epsilon_\mathrm{rel}\, |y|$$
  • Pure relative fails when $y$ passes through zero: the target collapses and no step is small enough.
  • Pure absolute fails when you do not know the scale of $y$ in advance.
  • Which $y$ goes into the target, $y^{(1)}$ or $y^{(2)}$? A free choice; they differ only at the order we are estimating.

Systems: one $h$, many errors

For $N_\mathrm{eq}$ coupled equations, every component $y_i$ has its own $\varepsilon_i$ and target — but there is only one $h$. Collapse them into a scalar:

$$\mathrm{err} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_\mathrm{eq}} \sum_{i=0}^{N_\mathrm{eq} - 1} \left(\frac{\varepsilon_i} {\varepsilon_{\mathrm{target},i}}\right)^2}$$
  • Each term is the error of one component in units of its own target.
  • $\mathrm{err} < 1$ means every component is (on RMS average) within tolerance: the step is good.
The RMS norm is a convention: the max over components is also used and slightly stricter. Anything that reduces the vector of ratios to one number monotonically works.

The safety factor

In terms of the scalar error, the update becomes

$$h' = S\, h \left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{err}}\right)^{1/5}$$
  • $S$ is a safety factor, slightly below 1; in practice $S = 0.9$ works well.
  • Why not $S = 1$? The $h^5$ law is only the leading term. Aiming exactly at $\mathrm{err} = 1$ means half the steps land just above it.

A rejected step throws away 11 evaluations of $f$. Undershooting the step size by 10% is far cheaper than rejecting.

The full algorithm

  1. From $(t, y)$, compute $y^{(1)}$ (one step of $h$) and $y^{(2)}$ (two steps of $h/2$).
  2. For each component, form $\varepsilon_i = |y_i^{(1)} - y_i^{(2)}|$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{target},i} = \epsilon_\mathrm{abs} + \epsilon_\mathrm{rel}\, |y_i|$.
  3. Combine into the scalar $\mathrm{err}$ (previous slide).
  4. If $\mathrm{err} < 1$: accept, advance $t \to t + h$ with $y^{(2)}$. Otherwise: reject, stay at $t$.
  5. Either way, set $h' = S\, h\, (\mathrm{err})^{-1/5}$ and continue. After an accepted step this usually grows $h$.

Rejections are not failures — they are the controller braking when the solution speeds up.

Practical refinements

  • Do not grow $h$ on the step right after a rejection; and cap the growth (e.g. at $5\times$) so one flat stretch cannot fling $h$ to infinity.
  • An empirically more stable update keeps some memory of the previous step: $$h' = S\, h\, (\mathrm{err})^{-0.7/5}\, (\mathrm{err}_\mathrm{prev})^{0.4/5}$$
  • Use an embedded method to cut the 11 evaluations per trial (Part IV).
  • Use interpolation to produce output at regular $t$ values even though the steps are irregular (dense output — next lecture).
The 0.7/5 and 0.4/5 exponents are quoted, not derived: this is a PI controller from control theory (Gustafsson). If pressed: the second factor is the "integral" memory term, and it damps the oscillations in h that the bare rule produces when the error hovers near 1. Numerical Recipes 17.2 discusses it.

Part III · The example

The integrator finds the timescale

Setting up the run

The chirped pulse from Part I is the solution of

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \omega\, e^{-t^2/a^2} \left(1 - \frac{2t^2}{a^2}\right) \cos\big(e^{-t^2/a^2}\, \omega t\big)$$
  • Adaptive RK4 by step doubling, exactly the algorithm of Part II.
  • $\epsilon_\mathrm{abs} = \epsilon_\mathrm{rel} = 10^{-6}$, $\;S = 0.9$, initial $h = 0.1$, from $t = -20$ to $20$.
The right-hand side is just the chain-rule derivative of the known y(t): f depends only on t, so this ODE is secretly a quadrature and the exact answer is available to measure errors against. That is exactly why it makes a good demo.

The computed solution

  • Every accepted step is a dot, drawn on the analytic solution. Right: a zoom into the center of the burst.
  • The dots land on the curve everywhere — and they crowd into the burst and spread out over the wings.
In the zoom there are roughly 18 steps per oscillation; in the wings a single step covers more than 2 units of t. This is the qualitative picture; the h(t) history on the next slide is the same information made quantitative, and the error comparison two slides on puts numbers on "land on the curve" (max deviation 4e-7).

The step size finds the physics

  • 378 accepted steps, 85 rejected, 5093 evaluations of $f$. The accepted $h$ spans $0.014$ to $2.6$ — a factor of 180.
  • Nobody told the integrator where the burst is. The error estimate discovered it.
The minimum h = 0.014 is about 1/20 of the local period 0.31 at t = 0: the controller resolves each oscillation with ~20 steps. The rejections cluster where the timescale is shrinking (73 of the 85 happen in |t| < 8, on the way into the burst): the controller only learns the error after trying a step, so it brakes late whenever the solution speeds up. The small wiggles in h track the oscillation phase; the spiky envelope mirrors the pulse shape.

Was it worth it?

  • Fixed-step RK4 with the same 5093 evaluations: max error $5\times10^{-5}$, versus $4\times10^{-7}$ adaptive — 140 times worse.
  • To match the adaptive error it needs about 19,400 evaluations: 4 times the cost.

Fixed $h$ wastes accuracy in the wings (error $10^{-10}$ where $10^{-6}$ would do) and still blows the budget in the burst. Adaptivity spends the error budget uniformly.

Part IV · Embedded methods

An error estimate for free

The cost of knowing the error

  • Step doubling pays 11 evaluations per trial, and incur more evaluations if the step is rejected. The example costed 13.5 evaluations per accepted step against 4 for plain RK4.
  • The estimate came from comparing two methods of different accuracy on the same step. But nothing says the two methods must be built from different evaluations.
  • Embedded methods: two weighted averages of the same stages $k_i$, one of order $p$ and one of order $p - 1$. The error estimate then costs nothing extra.

An embedded Butcher tableau

An embedded tableau carries two bottom rows: the step is taken with $b_i$, and the second row $b_i^*$ gives a lower-order companion.

$$\begin{array}{c|ccccc} c_1 & & & & & \\ c_2 & a_{21} & & & & \\ c_3 & a_{31} & a_{32} & & & \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & & \\ c_s & a_{s1} & a_{s2} & \cdots & a_{s,s-1} & \\ \hline & b_1 & b_2 & \cdots & b_{s-1} & b_s \\ & b_1^* & b_2^* & \cdots & b_{s-1}^* & b_s^* \end{array}$$
$$\varepsilon = |y_{n+1} - y_{n+1}^*| = h \left|\sum_{i=1}^{s} (b_i - b_i^*)\, k_i\right|$$
DERIVATION of the epsilon formula: both estimates are y_n + h sum b_i k_i with the same k_i, so the difference telescopes to h sum (b_i - b_i^*) k_i. One subtraction of already-computed numbers: that is the "free" in "error estimate for free".

Dormand-Prince 5(4)

Last lecture's Dormand-Prince tableau, now with its second row: a 5th-order method with an embedded 4th-order estimate.

$$\begin{array}{c|ccccccc} 0 & & & & & & & \\ 1/5 & 1/5 & & & & & & \\ 3/10 & 3/40 & 9/40 & & & & & \\ 4/5 & 44/45 & -56/15 & 32/9 & & & & \\ 8/9 & 19372/6561 & -25360/2187 & 64448/6561 & -212/729 & & & \\ 1 & 9017/3168 & -355/33 & 46732/5247 & 49/176 & -5103/18656 & & \\ 1 & 35/384 & 0 & 500/1113 & 125/192 & -2187/6784 & 11/84 & \\ \hline & 35/384 & 0 & 500/1113 & 125/192 & -2187/6784 & 11/84 & 0 \\ & 5179/57600 & 0 & 7571/16695 & 393/640 & -92097/339200 & 187/2100 & 1/40 \end{array}$$
  • Seven stages, but the last stage row equals the $b$ row: $k_7$ of this step is $k_1$ of the next, a property called FSAL ("first same as last"). Six new evaluations per accepted step, error estimate included.
  • This is the default method behind solve_ivp in scipy.
Compare costs on this lecture's example: step doubling paid 13.5 evaluations per accepted step; DP45 pays 6-7. Nobody derives these coefficients by hand; they are looked up. Order 5 needs six stages minimum (Lecture 7); the seventh buys FSAL and good error-estimate properties.

Cash-Karp 5(4)

Another embedded 5(4) pair, with friendlier coefficients:

$$\begin{array}{c|cccccc} 0 & & & & & & \\ 1/5 & 1/5 & & & & & \\ 3/10 & 3/40 & 9/40 & & & & \\ 3/5 & 3/10 & -9/10 & 6/5 & & & \\ 1 & -11/54 & 5/2 & -70/27 & 35/27 & & \\ 7/8 & 1631/55296 & 175/512 & 575/13824 & 44275/110592 & 253/4096 & \\ \hline & 37/378 & 0 & 250/621 & 125/594 & 0 & 512/1771 \\ & 2825/27648 & 0 & 18575/48384 & 13525/55296 & 277/14336 & 1/4 \end{array}$$
  • Six stages, no FSAL: all six evaluations are fresh each step. Developed with semi-stiff problems in mind; it behaves slightly better than Dormand-Prince there.

Using an embedded method

Only step 1 of the algorithm changes:

Compute $y^{(1)}$ and $y^{(2)}$ using one RK4 step of $h$ and two of $h/2$.

Compute $y^{(1)}$ and $y^{(2)}$ from the two bottom rows of the Dormand-Prince tableau.

  • Always advance with the 5th-order $y^{(1)}$; the 4th-order $y^{(2)}$ exists only to be subtracted.
  • Everything else — targets, the scalar $\mathrm{err}$, accept/reject, $h' = S\, h\, (\mathrm{err})^{-1/5}$ — carries over unchanged.
Purists note the estimate measures the 4th-order solution's error while we advance the 5th-order one ("local extrapolation"), so the true error is smaller than epsilon: a conservative estimate, which is fine. Same spirit as advancing with y^(2) in step doubling.

Adaptive DP45, complete

  1. From $(t, y)$, compute the seven stages $k_1, \dots, k_7$; form $y^{(1)}$ (row $b$) and $y^{(2)}$ (row $b^*$).
  2. $\varepsilon_i = |y_i^{(1)} - y_i^{(2)}|$, $\;\varepsilon_{\mathrm{target},i} = \epsilon_\mathrm{abs} + \epsilon_\mathrm{rel}\, |y_i^{(1)}|$.
  3. Combine into the scalar $\mathrm{err}$.
  4. $\mathrm{err} < 1$: accept, advance with $y^{(1)}$, reuse $k_7$ as the next $k_1$. Otherwise reject. Either way $h' = S\, h\, (\mathrm{err})^{-1/5}$.

This loop, plus the refinements of Part II, is what production ODE solvers actually run.
